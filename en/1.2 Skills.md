# Skills

## Overview

Skills in **Opcode:// D10** consist of a number of individual skills grouped under broad categories. Depending on the circumstances, they may be used in conjunction with different Attributes. Generally speaking, the Attributes listed below are the ones you're actually gonna use in most situations.

In addition to Skills, you also have Specializations, which is the subgroup of a skill that applies under more specific circumstances.

With all of those being said, we might as well get you started with **Skills**. The following is the core skill table for **Opcode:// D10** without any extensions.

## Making Checks

To make a Skill Check, roll:

**1D10 + Key Attribute + Key Skill + Specialisation** against a **Difficulty**.

The Difficulty table is provided as follows:

| Difficulty | Modifier / Difficulty Value | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Easy** | 10 | Simple problems — generally something that is won’t be requiring a check at all, except that this time, apparently, it  somedoes. |
| **Normal** | 15 | The most common seen difficulty. Working out how a switch operates, lifting something roughly equal to your own body weight, or dealing with a task that someone of average competence within the relevant profession could handle — even when they are in internship. |
| **Hard** | 20 | Both you and your character agree that this is definitely won’t be easy, but definitely won’t be impossible either. |
| **Very Hard** | 25 | A task requiring a trained industry professional to deal with. |
| **Nearly Impossible** | 30 | Essentially *Mission: Impossible*. Even the most accomplished people in the industry are going to need a little bit of luck. |
| **Under Pressure** | +2 | The character is currently under some form of pressure. |
| **Untrained** | +5 | The character has not been trained to perform this task. This depends on the time period: in the 2020s, almost everyone knows how to operate a computer or drive a car, but in the ’90s, not so much. |

### Purchasing Skills

Under the **Opcode:// D10** rules, players purchase their starting Skills using a number of Skill Points equal to their **maximum Attribute × 5**, or using **the sum of all Attribute Points × 1.5 + 5**.

Before you start digging into what all these Skills actually do, we recommend buying your Skills first, then looking into them in greater detail over the following chapters — they’re more or less self-explanatory enough for point-buy purposes.

### Critical Successes and Critical Failures

When the you get the 10 out of an 1D10 , the check is treated as **Critical Success**.

Depending on the pacing you want, you may simply allow them to succeed automatically, or have them roll an additional **1D10** and add it to the result into the original check. Generally speaking, if that second die also rolls a **10**, we recommend simply declaring that A Winner Is the Character.

Narratively, this might represent some unexpected factor somehow worked in their favour — or they are just really fucking good.

When the **1D10 hits a 1** \*and\* the Skill Check **fails**, the character suffers a **Critical Failure**.

The character just somehow fucked it up in a slightly unexpected fashion, and generally won’t get another chance to attempt the same action in quite the same way for quite the short time. Broke the switch? Accidentally rm -rf —no-preserve-rooted the system? Could be either. Who knows.

### Purchasing Specialisations

You may spend **1 Skill Point to purchase 1 point into a Skill Specialisation which is also called Specializations for apparent reason we won’t be stating here**.

Specialization is written in format of Skill: Specialization(descriptor). When making a check to which a Specialisation applies, you receive its respective value as additional Specialisation bonus. On top of this, you gain a further **+1 bonus for every 2 points you have in that Specialisation**. 

Specialisations may also be applied to related fields. For example, if you are attempting to maintain a rifle, you may add your **Marksmanship: Rifle** to the relevant fine-manipulation check.

### Extended Checks

When making a check that will take a substantial amount of time — usually **more than an hour**, and occasionally several days — you need to make an **Extended Check**.

An Extended Check has three components: a **Check Interval**, a number of **Retries**, and a **Difficulty**.

If a check turns out to be more difficult than the character expected, you may also convert an ordinary check into an Extended Check with the same difficulty.

Make one roll when the check begins. After each **Check Interval**, make another roll and add its result to the existing total. Continue doing so until the character’s accumulated result reaches the required target, the character has ran out of retries, or until the character has spent longer than expected on the task.

If the character suffers a **Critical Failure** at any point, the Extended Check immediately ends and is considered a failure.
